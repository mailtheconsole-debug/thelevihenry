The GTM Field Manual

01 · The toolkit

Five decisions before you can sell on purpose

Before a founder can sell deliberately instead of by accident, five things have to be settled: who to sell to, how to talk about it, what to charge, where to find buyers, and which motion fits the product.

Ideal customer profile (ICP)

Precision beats breadth. An ICP isn't a broad segment ("mid-market SaaS") — it's a narrow description of whoever gets the most value, fastest, at the lowest cost to win and serve. Build it while you build the product, so it drives what you make, not just who you email.

Framework · RaceCapital's 9 parameters

Nine questions that define an ICP

  1. Industry — target verticals, and explicit exclusions
  2. Geography — where you focus
  3. Company size — individual, startup, SMB, or enterprise
  4. Budget — what the market already expects to pay
  5. Decision makers — the buyer versus the actual user
  6. Buying process — direct purchase or committee procurement
  7. Pain points — the specific problem you solve
  8. Business goals — the outcome they're chasing
  9. Technology — required integrations and existing stack

A real ICP comes from three signals (First Round Review): interviewing the people who feel the pain most, analysing which deals actually close fastest and biggest, and — most useful early — situational triggers that predict urgency ("companies with 20+ open remote roles" beats "large company"). "Spray and pray" with no ICP is the single most common early failure.

Positioning

April Dunford's core idea in Obviously Awesome: positioning isn't a tagline, it's the context you deliberately choose so a customer instantly understands why the product is great for them. Get it wrong and marketing, sales, and pricing all get harder downstream. The move that matters most: name the true competitive alternative — often a spreadsheet or "doing nothing," not a named competitor — then choose your frame of reference on purpose.

Pricing and packaging

The highest-leverage decision is the value metric — what you actually charge for (per seat, per usage unit, per transaction, per outcome). Get the metric right and you can survive getting almost everything else wrong. Sequence it: define the value metric and quantify segments first, then pick an order of magnitude ($10 vs $100 vs $1K), then refine tiers, then optimise. Keep discounts under ~20%, frame annual upsells in dollars not percentages, and treat pricing as a quarterly experiment, never "done."

Channel selection

Framework · Weinberg's Bullseye (Traction)

Find the channel that works, instead of guessing

  1. What's possible: brainstorm all 19 traction channels, to counter your bias toward the two or three you already know.
  2. What's probable: run cheap, fast tests on the two or three most promising — under ~$1,000 and one month each.
  3. What's working: once one clearly outperforms, go all-in and optimise it, instead of spreading across several "kind of working" ones.

Most startups don't die from a bad product. They die from never systematically finding a working distribution channel — traction deserves the same rigour as the product itself.

Crossing the chasm

Geoffrey Moore's adoption curve runs innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards. The chasm sits between early adopters (who buy on vision) and the early majority (who buy on proof), which is why products with real early enthusiasm often stall. Two ways across: the beachhead — dominate one narrow, winnable niche completely before expanding — and the whole product — pragmatists need the integrations, security, training, and support wrapped around the core, not just the core.

Choosing the motion

MotionFits when
Product-led (PLG)Truly self-serve, buyer = user, deal size under ~$10K
Sales-led (SLG)Complex product, high ACV, multi-stakeholder buying
Marketing-ledUsually a top-of-funnel layer feeding SLG or PLG
Community-ledAn existing vocal, technical audience to amplify

The pure categories are increasingly theoretical — the common real pattern is a hybrid: a simple self-serve product acquires users cheaply, then a sales layer monetises and expands upmarket. Watch four failure modes on that path: the free adopters aren't the budget holders, the free tier gives away too much to leave anything to sell, an aggressive sales layer erodes trust with the self-serve base, and running both motions at once overwhelms a small team.

02 · The tactics

The first-customers playbook

Zero to a real customer base, in three phases — and what actually changes at each one.

First 10 — founder-led, manual, unscalable

Paul Graham's Do Things That Don't Scale is the canonical statement of this phase: startups don't take off on their own; founders have to push them over the initial hump by hand.

Stripe

The founders integrated Stripe live on the spot on early users' laptops — the "Collison installation" — watching confusion happen in real time and fixing it, and cut payment integration to seven lines of code.

Airbnb

Cross-posted listings to Craigslist where no integration existed, emailed existing vacation-rental posters directly, and personally re-shot host photos when NYC listings underperformed — bookings rose 2–3x, and it became an official photography program.

Superhuman

Built a product-market-fit engine around one survey question — "how would you feel if you could no longer use this?" — and the 40% rule (40%+ "very disappointed" = durable PMF). They moved from 22% to 58% by studying the fans and removing blockers for fence-sitters.

First 100 — systematising founder-led sales

Ad-hoc hustle becomes a lightweight but real process — build good sales habits before a sales team exists. Put in place a validated ICP, a basic funnel with real stages, a repeatable discovery-call structure (open with purpose, benefit, check; excavate the impact of the pain; use the prospect's own words back), and disciplined pilots offered only when a specific objection needs one.

First 1,000+ — past founder-led sales

Close the first 10–20 customers personally before handing off, so the process is genuinely understood. The inflection is typically ~$1M ARR, or when lead volume exceeds one founder. Hire two reps at once, not one, so you can tell what's repeatable from what's one rep's quirk — and stay on as player-coach rather than exiting sales entirely.

03 · The diagnosis

Reading the founder and the startup

The startup's shape decides which motion fits; the founder's background predicts the blind spot.

Startup shapes

ShapeBuyerTypical early motion
Horizontal SaaSA functional buyer at almost any companySelf-serve/PLG or scaled inside sales
Vertical SaaSAn owner/operator in one industry's workflowFounder-led, high-touch, industry credibility
B2BA buying committeeDemo-and-close, content, outbound/ABM
MarketplaceTwo sides with different motivationsSeed the harder side (usually supply) first
Dev tools / APIThe developer, via a free tierDocs, SDKs, community — bottoms-up
Hardware / deep techIndustrial/institutional buyersFounder-led discovery for years; partnerships

Founder types

Technical vs commercial. Technical founders tend to over-build the product and under-invest in distribution — the fix is to treat selling as problem-solving, the same discipline as debugging. Commercial founders carry the inverse risk: comfortable selling, but prone to over-promising or building something closer to consulting than a product.

First-time vs serial. First-timers often need explicit permission that founder-led sales is their job. Serial founders usually know that, but may lean on a prior playbook that doesn't match this company's buyer.

The reluctant seller. A founder who built something they're proud of and finds selling it beneath the "real work" — the tell-tale result is under-pricing, softened pitches, avoiding the direct ask, and delegating sales before there's a repeatable motion to hand off. There's no clean exit from sales for a founder, only a shift from doing all of it to stewarding it.

04 · The field guide

It changes by industry

Four structural patterns are more transferable than memorising a separate playbook per vertical.

  • The compliance-intensity spectrum: existential in fintech and healthtech → a trust signal the buyer audits you on in cybersecurity and legal tech → a product-shaping factor in HR tech, edtech, logistics → a late-stage checklist item in dev tools, martech, e-commerce, proptech.
  • The PLG-vs-committee spectrum: dev tools sit at the pure-PLG end; healthtech, legal tech, and edtech at the pure-committee end, where a stalled "no decision" is the real risk.
  • Distribution-as-moat is vertical-specific: channel/MLS relationships in proptech, app-store marketplaces in e-commerce, carrier-network breadth in logistics, open-source community in dev tools. Knowing which lever exists in a founder's vertical is half the advice.
  • Fiscal and seasonal calendars are underrated: the school fiscal year in edtech, holiday code freezes in e-commerce, the spring/summer season in proptech, Q3/Q4 budgets in HR tech all impose hard timing constraints.
