Sales development is one of the few careers where you can start remotely, skip the degree, and still be making real money within a year. Entry-level SDRs average around $55,000 a year, and OTE (base plus commission) at software companies regularly clears $80,000 to $100,000. No CS degree required. No four years of dues-paying in an office.

Most people who try to break in do it backwards. They fire off two hundred applications on Indeed and LinkedIn and wait. A handful get an interview. Almost none get an offer, and they walk away thinking the market is broken.

The market isn't broken. The approach is. Here's what actually works, and what the job looks like once you're in it.

## What an SDR actually does

A Sales Development Rep sits at the front of the sales process. You're not closing deals. You're starting conversations.

Your job is to find the right companies, get in front of the right people at those companies, and book qualified meetings for the account executives who do the closing. That's it. Find the fit, start the conversation, hand off the meeting.

Do that well and consistently and you become hard to replace, because pipeline is the one thing every sales org is short on. Companies can survive a slow quarter from one AE. They can't survive an empty pipeline.

## Why SDR is a smart first move into tech

**It's remote by default.** The job is calls, email, and LinkedIn. None of that requires you to be in an office, which is part of why remote SDR postings have grown faster than almost any other entry-level sales role over the last few years.

**It pays for output, not credentials.** Nobody in a hiring loop is going to ask where you went to school. They want to know if you can get fifteen strangers on the phone this week and turn three of them into meetings.

**It's the fastest on-ramp in tech sales.** SDR is the standard first rung on the ladder to Account Executive, sales manager, and eventually VP of Sales or revenue leadership, some of the highest-paid roles in the industry. Most AEs and sales leaders started exactly where you're starting now.

## The five skills that actually get you hired

You don't need product knowledge yet. Nobody expects that on day one. What you need to prove is that you can do the actual mechanics of the job:

1. **Prospecting.** Building a clean, targeted list instead of blasting everyone with a pulse. Knowing who to go after and why.
2. **Outreach that gets replies.** Writing a short, specific message tied to a real problem the prospect has, whether it's a cold call, an email, or a LinkedIn DM.
3. **Discovery.** Asking questions that reveal whether someone actually has the problem your product solves, instead of pitching blind.
4. **Handling objections.** Staying in the conversation after "not interested" without turning pushy or scripted.
5. **Follow-through.** Tracking who you've talked to and knowing exactly when to circle back.

None of that takes experience. It takes reps, the same way a jump shot or a golf swing does.

## How to start with zero experience

The fastest way in isn't more applications. It's proof. Give a hiring manager something they can actually look at instead of another resume that says "motivated self-starter."

- **Pick one industry and learn how it talks.** Who buys the product, why they buy it, and what they complain about when it doesn't work.
- **Write real outreach.** Draft the actual emails and LinkedIn messages you'd send if you had the job. This becomes your portfolio.
- **Practice out loud.** Record yourself running a mock cold call, listen back, fix one thing, run it again. Repeat until it doesn't sound rehearsed anymore.
- **Show your work when you apply.** Instead of claiming you can prospect, attach the message you'd send to their target buyer. That single move puts you ahead of almost everyone else in the stack.

Understand the role, put in the reps, and be able to prove both. That combination beats a polished resume every time, because most candidates still show up with nothing but the resume.
