I recently watched a video from a Meta ads specialist who paid a reported six figures to spend one day with Alex Hormozi. They talked about business in general, but a big chunk of the day went to Facebook and Instagram ads.

He came away with three lessons. I want to walk through them here, and add my own angle, because the third one is something I lived every single day as an SDR long before I ever touched Ads Manager.

## 1. Feed Meta more creative, and more kinds of creative

About a year ago Meta rolled out a new ad retrieval system called Andromeda. In plain terms, it got much better at deciding which specific person should see which specific ad.

The old approach grouped people into interest buckets and showed everyone in a bucket roughly the same thing. Now Meta can match individual people to the individual ads they are most likely to respond to.

A lot of advertisers say their results got worse after the change. According to the video, Hormozi said the opposite: results across his portfolio of companies are better than ever. The catch is the workload. His teams are reportedly producing dozens of new ads every week, and he admitted he finds it boring.

That tells you where the real bottleneck sits now. The old rule of "no more than six ads per ad set" is outdated. You can run 20, 30, or 50 ads at once. The limit is how much *good* creative you can actually make.

Variety matters as much as volume. Every format and style reaches a different kind of person:

- Customer testimonials
- You, the founder, talking straight to camera
- Product or service demos
- UGC and creator partnerships
- Static images, carousels, and short videos

If every ad you run looks the same, you are only speaking to the slice of your market that responds to that one style. Everyone else scrolls past.

**What this looks like for a small business:** block out one afternoon a week to record. Take one strong idea and shoot it five different ways. A testimonial, a quick demo, a talking-head version, a static quote, a carousel. Now you have five ads from one idea.

If your results dipped after Andromeda, treat it as an opening. Your competitors are struggling with the same change, and the ones who crack creative volume first get the advantage.

## 2. Your numbers have to be true

Hormozi apparently leans on data for almost every decision. Should we do this or that? Look at the numbers.

That only works if the numbers are accurate. When you decide which ad to kill based on cost per lead or return on ad spend, bad tracking means bad calls. You end up switching off the ads that were actually working and feeding budget to the ones that weren't.

Before you optimize anything, check the basics:

- Your Pixel and Conversions API are installed and firing
- Each conversion event fires once, on the right page, at the right moment
- The leads Meta reports roughly match the leads showing up in your inbox or CRM

If those three don't line up, fix that first. Everything else sits on top of it.

## 3. Tell Meta what a *good* lead looks like

This is the lesson the video called the most important, and I agree.

When I was cold calling as an SDR, I spent a lot of hours on leads that were never going to buy. Wrong budget, wrong timing, wrong industry. From the marketing dashboard, every one of those looked like a win. A lead is a lead.

Meta sees it the same way. It knows someone filled out your form. It has no idea your team looked at that person and decided they weren't a fit. So it goes and finds you more people just like them, and the cheapest people to reach are usually the least qualified.

The fix is to qualify people on the form itself and only reward Meta for the good ones.

**If you send traffic to your website:**

1. Add a qualifying question to your form. Budget is the classic one ("What's your budget?" with a few ranges), but timeline, location, industry, or company size all work.
2. Send qualified people to a thank-you page that fires its own conversion event, something like *Submit Application*.
3. Send everyone else to a separate, polite page that explains you may not be the right fit yet. No conversion event fires there.
4. In your ad set, optimize for the qualified event only.

**If you use Meta's instant forms:**

1. Turn on conditional logic and add a multiple choice question with your qualifying ranges.
2. For answers that don't qualify, set the form to close and show an end page for non-leads. Those people never register as leads.
3. For answers that qualify, let them submit and show your normal thank-you page.

Two extra upgrades worth turning on in instant forms: choose the **Higher intent** form type, and require **phone number verification by one-time SMS code**. People have to give you a real number, which quietly filters out anyone who isn't serious.

**If you sell products online:** once your account has some purchase data, switch your ad set from *maximize number of conversions* to *maximize value of conversions*. Otherwise Meta treats a small order and a big order as equally good, and goes after the cheap ones.

Then change how you judge your own results. Stop celebrating cost per lead. Watch cost per *qualified* lead, or cost per sale.

On day one this setup barely changes anything. Give it 30 to 90 days and it compounds, because every qualified lead teaches the system a little more about who you actually want.

## The thread running through all three

Each of these lessons comes down to the same thing: Meta is a very good student, and it learns exactly what you teach it. Show it one kind of ad and it finds one kind of person. Give it bad data and it learns the wrong lessons. Reward every lead and it brings you every lead.

Teach it well and it works hard for you.
